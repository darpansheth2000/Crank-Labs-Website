AI Strategy Roadmap for SMEs: A Practical 90-Day Plan

A week-by-week AI strategy roadmap for businesses of 20 to 500 people: use-case scoring, data readiness checklist, governance, training and how to measure ROI.

The short answer

An AI strategy roadmap for an SME is a short, sequenced plan that takes a business from scattered AI ideas to one or two working systems with measured results. A practical version runs about 90 days: two weeks to diagnose, two to score and choose use cases, six to design, build and pilot, and the rest to roll out, train staff, set governance and decide what comes next.

Most small and mid-sized businesses do not need a thick AI strategy document. They need an AI strategy roadmap that fits on one page, names a first project, and gets something working inside a quarter. This guide sets out a practical 90-day AI plan for a business of 20 to 500 people: what to do each week, how to score use cases, what to check about your data and systems, how to govern and train, and how to measure the return using your own numbers.

What is an AI strategy roadmap, and what is it not?

An AI strategy roadmap answers four questions: which business problems AI will address first, in what order, who owns each one, and how you will know it worked. It is tied to revenue, cost or time saved, not to a list of tools.

It is not:

  • A technology wish list. "Deploy a chatbot, try a forecasting model, explore computer vision" is not a roadmap.
  • A multi-year transformation plan. For an SME, anything beyond the next two or three projects will change once the first one teaches you something.
  • An IT project. The people who own sales, operations and finance must own the roadmap. IT and any outside partner support them.

If your business needs deeper change to how sales, operations or finance run before AI can help, read our business transformation framework alongside this plan.

Who should own the AI roadmap?

A 90-day AI implementation plan needs a small, named team. In most SMEs that means:

  • Executive sponsor: the owner, CEO or COO. Sets priorities, removes blockers, signs off the go or no-go decisions.
  • Process owner: the manager of the function being changed, for example the head of accounts or the sales manager. Owns the new process and reviews the AI's work in the first weeks.
  • Champion: one or two people from the team doing the work. They test early versions and tell you what breaks.
  • Systems contact: whoever manages your software and access, in-house or an IT provider.
  • Delivery partner: an internal developer or an outside AI consulting firm that designs and builds.

Budget roughly a few hours a week from the process owner and champion. If you cannot free that time, postpone the roadmap. An AI adoption strategy without operational owners stalls quietly.

The timeline is realistic because the first project is narrow. A focused agent or workflow, such as WhatsApp lead response or invoice entry, is typically live in two to four weeks of build. The rest of the 90 days goes on choosing well, preparing properly and making the change stick.

Weeks 1 to 2: how do you find the right AI use cases?

Do not start by asking "where can we use AI?". Start by asking where time and money leak. Three activities give you most of what you need.

Walk the process

Sit with the people who handle enquiries, orders, invoices, approvals and reports. Ask them to show you, not tell you. Note every place where information is re-typed, copied between systems, chased by phone or waits in someone's inbox.

Collect real samples

Gather a few weeks of enquiries, a batch of invoices, CRM exports and the spreadsheets people actually use. These show volumes, formats and edge cases, and they tell you early whether the data is usable.

Build the long list

Write each candidate as a sentence: "Supplier invoices arriving by email are keyed into the accounting software by two people every day." Add rough weekly volume and who does it. A long list for a mid-sized business usually covers enquiry handling, quoting, follow-ups, document processing, scheduling, reporting and internal requests.

Weeks 3 to 4: how do you score and prioritise AI use cases?

Score every candidate from 1 to 5 on four factors. The descriptions below keep scoring consistent between different people.

FactorScore 1Score 3Score 5
ValueRare task, little time or revenue at stakeWeekly task, noticeable time or some revenue at stakeDaily, high-volume task or directly tied to lost revenue
Effort (reverse scored)Many systems, custom development, unclear processTwo or three systems, some integration workOne or two systems with good access, clear process
Data readinessData scattered, inconsistent or mostly in people's headsData exists but needs cleaning or a standard formatData is digital, consistent and accessible today
Risk (reverse scored)Errors would hurt customers, patients or finances and are hard to catchErrors are recoverable with a review stepErrors are low impact and easy to spot

Because effort and risk are reverse scored, a higher total is always better. Add the four scores. Then apply two rules before ranking: any use case scoring 1 on data readiness waits until the data is fixed, and any use case scoring 1 on risk needs a mandatory human approval step built into the design.

Here is an illustrative scoring for a generic 150-person kitchen and interior manufacturer:

Candidate use caseValueEffortData readinessRiskTotal
WhatsApp enquiry response and showroom booking544417
Supplier invoice entry into the accounting software445316
Weekly production status report for management333514
Demand forecasting for raw materials421310

In this example the enquiry agent goes first, invoice entry second, and forecasting waits because the data is not ready. That is a typical pattern: the exciting use case is rarely the right first one. Before the build starts, take a baseline for the chosen use cases, such as average response time to an enquiry or hours spent on invoice entry each week.

Is your data and integration ready? A checklist

Run this checklist in weeks 5 and 6. Every "no" is either a task to fix before the build or a reason to change the design.

  • Do we know exactly which systems the workflow touches (CRM, accounting software, email, WhatsApp, spreadsheets, databases)?
  • Can each system be accessed through an API, an integration or a reliable export?
  • Are the logins and accounts in the company's name, not an individual's?
  • Is the data the AI needs digital and in a consistent format?
  • Are customer, supplier and product records reasonably free of duplicates?
  • Do we have a set of past cases, with correct outcomes, to test against?
  • Have we written down the edge cases and exceptions staff handle today?
  • Do we know which data is personal or sensitive, and which data protection laws apply?
  • Is it agreed what the AI may read, what it may write, and what it may never do?
  • Is there a named person to approve AI actions involving money, health or legal commitments?
  • Is there somewhere to log every AI action so it can be reviewed?

Weeks 5 to 10: how do you design, build and pilot?

The design should be a short written description of the new process, step by step, including where the AI acts, where it hands to a person and who approves what. Keep it to a page or two so the process owner can actually check it.

During the build, insist on three habits:

  1. Build on real data. Test against past enquiries or invoices with known outcomes before any customer or ledger sees the result.
  2. Start supervised. In the pilot, the AI drafts and a person approves. Remove approvals only where the record shows the AI is reliably right and mistakes would be cheap.
  3. Review daily, then weekly. Short reviews of what the AI did, what it got wrong and what it handed over. Fix rules and prompts as you go.

Weeks 11 to 13: how do you handle governance and team training?

Governance that fits an SME

You do not need a committee. You need a few written rules that everyone knows:

  • Approved tools: which AI tools staff may use, and which data may never be pasted into public tools.
  • Approval rules: which actions need a person to sign off, especially anything financial, clinical or contractual.
  • Access: each AI system gets only the permissions its workflow needs.
  • Logging and review: every action is logged and a named person reviews a sample regularly.
  • Incidents: how staff report a wrong answer or action, and who fixes it.
  • Data protection: alignment with the laws that apply, such as India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 or the UAE Personal Data Protection Law.

Training by role

  • Front-line staff: what the system does, how to spot a mistake, how to take over a conversation or correct an entry.
  • Process owners: how to read the logs, adjust rules and track the measures.
  • Leadership: what the results review shows and how to choose the next use case.

Training is where adoption is won. A system that staff do not trust gets worked around within weeks.

How do you measure AI ROI without making up numbers?

The only numbers that matter are your own. Measure before and after, using the same method, on the same process.

MeasureHow to take the baselineHow to measure after
Staff time on the taskTime a sample of tasks and multiply by weekly volumeTime the remaining manual work, including reviews and corrections
Response time to enquiriesTimestamps of first reply from WhatsApp or email historySame timestamps from the system logs
Missed or unanswered enquiriesCount enquiries with no reply within your target timeSame count after launch
Errors and reworkCount corrections in a sample periodCount corrections in the same length of period
Cycle timeDays from invoice receipt to posting, or from enquiry to quoteSame measure after launch
ConversionShare of enquiries that became bookings or orders in a sample monthSame calculation, compared month to month

Then put it in plain money terms: hours saved per month multiplied by the loaded cost of those hours, plus any extra revenue from enquiries that would otherwise have been missed, minus the build fee spread over a sensible period and the monthly running costs. If the result is clearly positive, extend. If it is marginal, adjust. If it is negative after tuning, stop and move to the next use case. What are the most common mistakes in an AI adoption strategy?

  • Starting with the most impressive use case instead of the one with ready data.
  • Skipping the baseline, so nobody can prove the project worked.
  • Running the project from IT without an operational owner.
  • Removing human approval too early to save a little time.
  • Trying to automate a process that the team does not agree on.
  • Planning five projects at once rather than one at a time.
  • Treating launch as the finish line instead of the start of tuning.

Frequently asked questions

Is 90 days realistic for a first AI project in a small business?

Yes, if the first project is narrow. One workflow with clear volume, such as enquiry response or invoice entry, can be designed, built, piloted and rolled out within a quarter. The build itself typically takes two to four weeks. What makes timelines slip is choosing a use case with poor data or no operational owner.

Do we need a Chief AI Officer or a data team?

Not for most businesses of 20 to 500 people. You need an executive sponsor, a process owner for each use case, a champion from the team and a delivery partner, whether in-house or external. Specialist roles make sense later, once several AI systems are running and the volume of work justifies them.

How often should an AI roadmap be updated?

Review it at the end of every project and at least once a quarter. Each completed project teaches you about your data, your systems and how your team adopts change, and that should reshape the order of what comes next. A roadmap that stays fixed for a year has usually stopped being used.

Should we build our own AI tools or buy off-the-shelf software?

Use off-the-shelf features where they already exist in software you pay for. Build when the value depends on connecting several of your systems, on your own rules and data, or on a workflow no product covers well. Many SMEs end up with a mix: bought tools at the edges and custom integrations in the middle.
