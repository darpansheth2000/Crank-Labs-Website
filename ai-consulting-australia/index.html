How does AI bookkeeping automation work for Australian practices?

For accounting and bookkeeping firms, the pressure point is the sheer number of small, repetitive tasks around each BAS period. The work is not hard. It is constant, and it lands on the same people at the same time every quarter.

A typical automation for an Australian practice runs in six stages:

  1. Collection. Receipts, supplier bills and statements arrive by email, upload or phone photo. The system files each one against the right client and flags anything it cannot read.
  2. Extraction. Supplier, date, amounts and GST are read from each document, with a confidence check so unclear items go to a person instead of being guessed.
  3. Matching. Documents are matched to bank feed transactions already in the client's ledger, whether that is Xero, MYOB or QuickBooks Online, and a suggested coding is prepared from how that client's transactions have been coded before.
  4. Review. A bookkeeper sees the draft entry, the source document and the suggestion side by side, then approves, edits or rejects it. Nothing is posted until a person says so.
  5. Chasing. Clients with missing documents get a polite email listing exactly what is outstanding, and the practice can see who has responded.
  6. Preparation. The reviewed figures feed the working papers the practice already uses to prepare the BAS.

The AI does not lodge anything and does not give tax advice. Lodgement stays with the registered tax or BAS agent, and professional judgement on coding, GST treatment and anything unusual stays with your team. What changes is that bookkeepers spend their time reviewing and advising rather than keying. A worked example: a 25-person accounting practice in Melbourne

Picture a 25-person practice in Melbourne with a mix of tax, bookkeeping and advisory clients, most of them small businesses on Xero and a handful on MYOB. Documents arrive through a shared inbox, a client portal and individual staff email addresses. Bookkeepers download attachments, rename them, key the details and match them to bank feed lines. In the weeks before each BAS due date, partners help with data entry and the advisory work waits.

A sensible sequence for that practice:

  1. Diagnose. A free working session maps how documents arrive, who touches them and where they stall. Often the biggest delay is not the keying but the chasing.
  2. First build. Automated capture and extraction from the shared inbox, matched against bank feeds, with a review screen for bookkeepers. A focused workflow like this is typically live in two to four weeks.
  3. Second build. Automated chasing for missing documents, tied to each client's BAS cycle, so reminders start early instead of in the final week.
  4. Scale. Once the first change pays for itself, the practice moves to the next function, such as onboarding new clients or preparing monthly management reports.

Each step is measured against the old way before the next begins: hours spent per client each quarter, documents outstanding at each stage, and how many draft entries a reviewer had to correct. The practice keeps its ledger software, its portal and its working papers.

How do the Privacy Act and the APPs apply when work happens offshore?

The Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles set the rules for how many Australian organisations collect, use, store and disclose personal information. Some small businesses are exempt, but health service providers are generally covered whatever their size, and accounting practices hold sensitive financial information about their clients, so it is sensible to design as if the principles apply.

Because Crank Labs works from outside Australia, cross-border disclosure matters. Under the APPs, an organisation that discloses personal information to a recipient overseas is generally expected to take reasonable steps to make sure that recipient handles it in line with the principles, and it can remain accountable for what the recipient does. Even when your data is stored in Australia, our team accesses it from Dubai or Mumbai, so treat the engagement as an overseas disclosure when you assess it. That shapes how we build:

  • Storage in Australia. Systems can run on your own cloud account in an Australian region, so documents and records stay there rather than on our infrastructure.
  • Least access. Each workflow reaches only the data it needs. A chasing agent sees which documents are missing, not the full ledger.
  • Human approval. For finance and healthcare, a person approves before anything is sent or posted.
  • Logs. Actions are recorded, which helps if you ever need to investigate an incident or assess it under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme.
  • Clear terms. What we can access, from where and for what purpose is written into the engagement, so your privacy policy and client disclosures can reflect it.

This is engineering practice, not legal advice. Talk to your privacy or legal adviser about your own obligations, particularly if you handle health information or tax file numbers.

How to choose AI consultants in Australia

Whether you are comparing AI consulting in Sydney, an AI automation agency in Melbourne or an offshore team like ours, ask every firm the same questions:

  • Where exactly will our data be stored, and from which countries will your team access it?
  • Can you show the workflow running on our real documents or enquiries, not a demo dataset?
  • How will you connect to Xero, MYOB, QuickBooks Online or our CRM, and what happens when that connection fails?
  • Where does a person approve, and can we see the audit trail?
  • Who supports the system after launch, and in which hours?
  • What will you measure to prove it worked, and against what baseline?

Red flags: a fixed price before anyone has looked at your processes, vague answers about where data goes, an AI that posts to the ledger or messages clients without review, a proposal to replace software your team already knows, and no plan for who maintains the system next year.
